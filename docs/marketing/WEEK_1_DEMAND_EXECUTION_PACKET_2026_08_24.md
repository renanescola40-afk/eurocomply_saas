## Publish copy — English

An AI inventory that only lists tool names is not enough to run governance.

“ChatGPT — Marketing”
“Copilot — Engineering”
“AI assistant — Support”

That may be a useful discovery list.

But an operational inventory needs more context:

**System / use case**
What is the organization actually using AI to do?

**Owner**
Who is accountable for keeping the record current?

**Provider**
Which vendor, service or model is involved?

**Deployment and geography**
Where and how is it used?

**People and data context**
Who uses it, who may be affected, and what data context matters?

**Review state**
Has risk, transparency, vendor or policy review happened?

**Rationale**
Why did the organization reach its current conclusion?

**Evidence**
Which documents, decisions or records support that conclusion?

**Next review**
What date or material change should trigger another look?

A list tells you that AI exists.

A governance inventory tells you enough to assign responsibility and decide what needs attention.

RISCK COMPLY is being built around that operating model: systems, owners, risk context, evidence and review connected in one workspace.

No legal shortcut. No “one-click compliant” promise.

# WEDNESDAY 26 AUG — FOUNDER POST 2

Campaign: `FOUNDER-001`
Theme: Inventory ≠ tools list
Audience: Compliance + Security + Procurement + AI leaders

## Publish copy — English

A surprising amount of “AI inventory” work starts with this question:

> Which AI tools do we use?

Useful question.

But it is not the governance question.

The governance question is:

> Which AI **systems and use cases** are operating in the organization — and what do we know about each one?

One vendor can support several use cases.

One use case can involve several providers.

The same AI service can have very different governance implications depending on:

- the business process
- the people affected
- the data involved
- the level of automation
- the country or market
- the owner
- the review history

That is why a vendor list eventually breaks down.

The unit of governance has to carry enough context for someone else to review the decision later.

My minimum useful record would answer:

1. What is the system/use case?
2. Why is it being used?
3. Who owns it?
4. Which provider is involved?
5. What review has happened?
6. What evidence exists?
7. When should it be reviewed again?

If your AI inventory already exists, what field has turned out to be the hardest to keep current?

# FRIDAY 28 AUG — FOUNDER POST 3

Campaign: `FOUNDER-001`
Theme: Spreadsheet breakpoint
Audience: Compliance, Security, Legal, Procurement, operations

## Publish copy — English

Spreadsheets are not the enemy of AI governance.

For a small scope, they can be exactly the right tool.

The problem starts when the process becomes relational and continuous.

A spreadsheet becomes expensive when:

- one AI system has several owners or reviewers
- one vendor supports several systems
- evidence expires or changes
- a customer asks for proof
- procurement needs another review
- an owner leaves the company
- a new country or business process is added
- the system changes but the old decision stays in the sheet
- nobody is sure which version is current

At that point, the problem is not “Excel is bad”.

The problem is that governance now has:

**relationships + ownership + state + history + recurring review**.

That is software-shaped work.

A useful test:

If I asked your team today:

> “Show me every AI system due for review, the accountable owner, the supporting evidence and what materially changed since the last decision.”

How many tabs, inboxes and folders would you need to open?

That answer tells you more about governance maturity than the number of policies in the folder.
