## Post 01 — 3 September — The AI Act is operational now

The EU AI Act conversation has moved from “coming soon” to operational reality.

From 2 August 2026, the Act became generally applicable and new transparency obligations started applying, while some high-risk requirements still follow later dates.

For companies using AI, the useful question is no longer just:

“What does the law say?”

It is:

- Which AI systems are we actually using?
- Who owns each one?
- Which providers and data flows are involved?
- What risk rationale have we documented?
- What evidence could we show a buyer, board or reviewer today?

Governance starts with visibility.

RISCK COMPLY is being built around that operating layer: inventory, ownership, risk, policy and evidence — without pretending software replaces legal counsel.

## Post 02 — 7 September — Start with inventory

You cannot govern an AI system that nobody has formally recorded.

A useful AI inventory should answer more than “which tools do we use?”

For every system, capture:

- business use case;
- owner;
- department;
- provider;
- data context;
- affected users or decisions;
- current risk assessment;
- policies and evidence linked to it.

That turns AI governance from a slide deck into an operating process.

The first maturity milestone is not a perfect policy.

It is knowing where AI exists inside the company.

## Post 04 — 11 September — Article 50 transparency

AI transparency is now an operational requirement, not just a UX preference.

Article 50 of the EU AI Act applies from 2 August 2026 and includes transparency duties for specific AI interactions and generated or manipulated content scenarios.

That creates practical questions for product and compliance teams:

- Where does a user interact directly with AI?
- Is the disclosure clear at the right moment?
- Are synthetic-content obligations relevant to the use case?
- Who owns the evidence that the control exists?

A regulation becomes manageable when each obligation has an owner, a system and evidence attached to it.

#EUAIAct #Article50 #AITransparency #ProductCompliance

## Post 05 — 14 September — Procurement asks for proof

AI governance increasingly becomes a sales issue before it becomes a regulator issue.

An enterprise buyer may ask:

- Where do you use AI?
- Which vendors process data?
- How do you classify AI risk?
- Who approves new AI use cases?
- What controls exist around human oversight?
- Can you show evidence?

If the answer requires five people and six folders, the problem is operational.

Good governance reduces that friction by creating a repeatable record before the questionnaire arrives.

Prepare evidence before procurement asks for it.

## Post 07 — 18 September — Every AI system needs an owner

An AI register without ownership is just a list.

For every material AI use case, someone should be able to answer:

“I am responsible for keeping this record current.”

Ownership makes the next controls possible:

- risk review;
- vendor review;
- policy acknowledgement;
- evidence collection;
- remediation;
- periodic reassessment.

The governance question is not only “what AI do we use?”

It is “who is accountable for the record when something changes?”

## Post 08 — 21 September — Do not confuse staged timelines with no action

The EU AI Act does not switch every obligation on at the same date.

Current European Commission guidance reflects staged application dates, including later dates for major high-risk-system requirements.

That is not a reason to wait.

Inventory, ownership, AI literacy, transparency analysis, vendor mapping and evidence discipline are useful before a high-risk deadline arrives.

The best time to discover your AI estate is not during a procurement review or regulatory deadline.

Prepare early — without manufacturing false urgency.

## Post 09 — 23 September — What belongs in an AI evidence pack?

An AI governance evidence pack should make a reviewer’s job easier.

A practical pack can connect:

- system description and purpose;
- accountable owner;
- provider/vendor information;
- risk rationale;
- applicable internal policies;
- human oversight notes where relevant;
- approvals and review dates;
- remediation actions;
- supporting documents and logs.

The value is not simply generating another PDF.

The value is being able to trace every statement back to a maintained operating record.

## Post 10 — 25 September — Third-party AI is still your governance problem

Using a third-party AI provider does not make governance disappear.

Teams still need to understand:

- what the tool is used for;
- which data enters the workflow;
- who approved the use case;
- which provider is involved;
- what contractual or privacy evidence exists;
- what happens if the provider or model changes.

Vendor review and AI inventory should not live in separate universes.

A useful governance system connects them.

#VendorRisk #ThirdPartyRisk #AIGovernance #Privacy

## Post 11 — 28 September — AI literacy should connect to real systems

AI literacy is stronger when it is tied to the AI systems people actually use.

Generic training is useful.

But operational maturity improves when employees also understand:

- which AI uses are approved;
- what data should not be entered;
- when human review is required;
- where incidents or concerns are reported;
- which policy applies to the tool in front of them.

Training, policy and inventory should reinforce each other.

That is how governance becomes a daily operating habit rather than an annual slide deck.
