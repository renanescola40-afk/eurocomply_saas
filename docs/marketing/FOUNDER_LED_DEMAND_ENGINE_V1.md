## 5. Founder content pillars

### Pillar A — Category creation: Operational AI Governance

Core idea:

> AI governance is not the policy document. It is the operating system that keeps systems, owners, risk, evidence and review connected after the policy is written.

Angles:

- why AI governance fails after the first spreadsheet;
- inventory is not governance;
- policy is not execution;
- governance debt;
- ownership decay;
- stale evidence;
- shadow AI as an operating-data problem.

### Pillar B — EU AI Act precision

Core rule:

Make regulatory content more accurate than fear-based posts.

Angles:

- what changed after 2 August 2026;
- what did not suddenly become due;
- Article 50 practical questions;
- provider vs deployer role context;
- Germany KI-MIG;
- Spain AESIA/RIA guidance;
- difference between legal interpretation and operational implementation.

Every material regulatory claim should use an official source.

### Pillar C — Build in public, but evidence-first

Angles:

- why the Trust Center shows gaps instead of fake badges;
- why legal-compliance guarantee claims are dangerous;
- why we designed manual analytics consent;
- why ownership/history matter in an AI inventory;
- why product records should preserve rationale, not only status.

Do not expose secrets, security-sensitive architecture details or private customer information.

### Pillar D — Buyer problems

Angles:

- customer questionnaire arrives and six teams start chasing files;
- procurement asks which AI vendors are in use;
- board asks who owns AI risk;
- legal asks why a classification was made six months ago;
- AI owner leaves;
- a vendor changes its model/service;
- evidence is stale but still sitting in Drive.

Use hypothetical/typical scenarios clearly, never fake customer stories.

### Pillar E — Operator education

Practical checklists:

- fields every AI inventory needs;
- questions before approving a new AI use case;
- vendor AI review checklist;
- evidence quality checklist;
- governance handoff checklist;
- review trigger checklist.

These are the highest bridge to lead magnets and product.

## 7. 30-day founder-led sequence

### WEEK 1 — Own the operational-governance category

Post 1:

Hook:

> Most AI governance programs do not fail because the company has no policy. They fail because nobody can operate the policy after it is approved.

Body points:

- systems change;
- owners change;
- vendors change;
- evidence expires;
- review decisions lose context;
- spreadsheets split across teams.

CTA:

> What part of AI governance is hardest to keep current in your organization?

Post 2:

Hook:

> An AI inventory that only lists tool names is a software list, not a governance system.

Teach the operational fields:

- use case;
- owner;
- provider;
- role/context;
- data;
- review state;
- evidence;
- next review.

CTA -> AI Inventory Template when available.

Post 3:

Hook:

> There is a point where Excel stops being cheap.

Explain the breakpoint without insulting spreadsheets.

### WEEK 2 — Regulatory precision

Post 4:

> 2 August 2026 changed the EU AI Act operating environment. It did not make every AI system high-risk overnight.

Use official Commission sourcing.

Post 5:

> Provider vs deployer sounds like legal vocabulary until a team has to decide who owns the next action.

Connect role to inventory/workflow.

Post 6:

> Article 50 is not one generic disclosure rule.

Explain that obligations depend on system/context/role; link official guidance.

### WEEK 3 — Evidence and trust

Post 7:

> A PDF in Drive is not automatically governance evidence.

Explain relevance, freshness, linkage and owner.

Post 8:

> We deliberately do not put fake compliance badges on RISCK COMPLY.

Explain evidence-bound trust.

Post 9:

> The most useful question in a governance review may be: “Can we still explain why this decision was made six months later?”

### WEEK 4 — Buyer/operating problems

Post 10:

> A customer asks for your AI governance process. Which team answers first?

Map compliance/legal/security/procurement collaboration.

Post 11:

> Shadow AI is not only a security problem. It is also an inventory and ownership problem.

Post 12:

> If the person who owns an AI system leaves tomorrow, what governance context leaves with them?

CTA to structured ownership/inventory workflow.
