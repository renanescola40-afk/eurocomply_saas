## 2. Regulatory truth contract

Use only the following verified baseline unless newer official guidance supersedes it:

- Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026.
- Providers of systems intended to interact directly with natural persons may need to ensure users are informed that they are interacting with AI, subject to the Article 50 conditions/exceptions.
- Providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text have machine-readable marking/detectability obligations under Article 50(2), subject to scope and exceptions.
- Deployers have specific transparency duties in defined cases including emotion recognition/biometric categorisation, deep fakes, and certain AI-generated/manipulated public-interest text.
- A limited grace period to 2 December 2026 applies only to the Article 50(2) marking/detection obligation for systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026.
- Provider/deployer role must be assessed by system, use case and context. Do not say that every professional AI user is automatically a deployer for every purpose.
- Article 50 does not mean `label every use of AI`.

Primary evidence sources:

- European Commission — Guidelines on transparency obligations for providers and deployers of AI systems — 20 July 2026.
- European Commission — Transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act FAQ.
- AI Act Service Desk — Article 50 / enforcement timing.
- EUR-Lex — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 50.

# MONDAY 31 AUG — FOUNDER POST

Campaign: `FOUNDER-002`
Theme: Regulatory precision
Audience: Compliance, Legal, AI Governance, Security
CTA: conversation

## Publish copy — English

Article 50 is now applicable.

That does **not** mean every AI system needs the same label, disclosure or workflow.

The first operational question is not:

“Do we use AI?”

It is:

**What system is this, what is it doing, and what role do we have in this context?**

For one system, the relevant question may be whether a person is informed they are interacting with AI.

For another, it may be machine-readable marking of generated content.

For another, the deployer may need to disclose a deepfake, emotion-recognition use, or certain public-interest content.

This is why an AI inventory made only of vendor names is not enough.

Operational governance needs to connect:

system -> use case -> role -> owner -> transparency duty -> evidence -> review.

The regulation creates the obligation.

The hard part inside a company is making the obligation operable.

Question for governance teams: can you map those seven fields for each material AI use case today?

### Comment rule

If someone asks for legal classification, answer with the relevant official source and explain that facts/context matter. Do not classify their system from a LinkedIn comment.

# TUESDAY 1 SEP — COMPANY POST

Campaign: `EN-REG-002`
Theme: Article 50 decision map
CTA: guide / feature when publication gate permits; otherwise conversation

## Publish copy — English

**Article 50 is not one transparency rule.**

A practical first pass is to separate the questions:

1. Does the AI system interact directly with natural persons?
2. Does it generate synthetic audio, image, video or text?
3. Is a deployer using emotion recognition or biometric categorisation?
4. Is the content a deepfake?
5. Is AI-generated/manipulated text being published to inform the public on a matter of public interest?
6. Which entity is provider, deployer, or both in the relevant context?
7. What evidence shows the disclosure/marking/review actually happened?

The purpose of governance is to make those answers traceable by system and use case — not leave them inside a one-off memo.

RISCK COMPLY is designed to help teams organise that operating context: inventory, ownership, risk/review state, evidence and follow-up.

It does not replace qualified legal analysis or guarantee compliance.

# WEDNESDAY 2 SEP — FOUNDER POST

Campaign: `FOUNDER-002`
Theme: Provider vs deployer
CTA: conversation

## Publish copy — English

“Provider or deployer?” sounds like a definition question.

Inside a company, it becomes a data-model question.

You need to know:

- which AI system;
- which use case;
- who developed or placed it on the market;
- who uses it under whose authority;
- whether it has been modified or rebranded;
- what jurisdiction/context applies;
- who owns the decision internally;
- what evidence supports the conclusion.

A company can have different roles across different systems.

That is why a single company-level label like “we are a deployer” is often too coarse for operating governance.

The useful unit is the **system/use-case record**.

Role -> rationale -> owner -> evidence -> review date.

That is the difference between knowing a definition and being able to govern it.

# FRIDAY 4 SEP — BUYER PROBLEM POST

Campaign: `FOUNDER-002`
Theme: Evidence

## Publish copy — English

A disclosure is not governed just because someone added it once.

Six months later, can you answer:

- which AI system required it?
- which use case triggered it?
- who approved the wording?
- where it is implemented?
- when it was last reviewed?
- what changed since then?

That is the evidence problem behind AI transparency.

Compliance work becomes expensive when every review starts by reconstructing context from Slack, email and old documents.

The better model is continuous:

requirement -> owner -> implementation -> evidence -> review trigger -> history.

That is what operational AI governance should preserve.
