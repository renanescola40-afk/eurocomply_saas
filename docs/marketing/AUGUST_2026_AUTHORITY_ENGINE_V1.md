## 6. LINKEDIN CLAIM CLEANUP MATRIX

Public posts should be reviewed using the following rules.

### CLAIM A — “AI systems used in logistics, warehouse management, or automated inventory systems are classified as high-risk.”

Status: **REMOVE / REPLACE**

Why:

High-risk status under the AI Act depends on the system and use case defined by the Regulation, not a blanket industry label. Logistics or warehouse use alone is not enough to safely state that a system is high-risk.

Replacement:

> AI risk classification depends on the specific use case, role and legal criteria. Inventorying the system and documenting why a classification applies is more reliable than assigning risk based only on the department or industry using it.

### CLAIM B — “The AI Act applies to any professional use of AI, except purely personal use.”

Status: **REMOVE / REPLACE**

Why:

The Act contains scope rules, role-specific obligations, exclusions and phased application dates. “Any professional use” is too broad.

Replacement:

> Companies using or providing AI in the EU should determine whether they fall within the AI Act’s scope, identify their role for each system and map which obligations are actually applicable.

### CLAIM C — “The ultimate responsibility lies with your company even when the AI solution is provided by a third party.”

Status: **REMOVE / REPLACE**

Why:

Responsibilities vary by legal role and obligation. Providers, deployers, importers, distributors and other actors can have different duties.

Replacement:

> Third-party AI does not remove the need for governance. Organizations should identify their role, understand supplier responsibilities and document the obligations that remain with them.

### CLAIM D — generic “€35m / 7%” penalty used for all AI Act breaches

Status: **REWRITE**

Why:

Penalty ceilings vary by infringement. Article 99 distinguishes categories, and SMEs are subject to additional proportionality rules.

Replacement:

> AI Act penalty ceilings vary by type of infringement. Prohibited practices can carry the highest ceilings, while other operator and transparency obligations have different thresholds. Penalty language should always identify the relevant category rather than using one figure for every breach.

### CLAIM E — “continuous monitoring / frequent internal audits / mandatory reports” stated as universal obligations

Status: **REWRITE**

Why:

These concepts may apply in particular contexts, but should not be marketed as universal duties for every organization using AI.

Replacement:

> The practical governance burden depends on the system, use case and organization’s role. A defensible operating model starts with inventory, ownership, classification and evidence, then adds the controls required for the relevant obligations.

Use official EU sources before publishing regulatory content.

Verified current milestones for the campaign:

- 1 August 2024 — AI Act entered into force.
- 2 February 2025 — general provisions on definitions/AI literacy and prohibited practices began to apply.
- 2 August 2025 — governance and GPAI obligations began to apply.
- 2 August 2026 — the majority of rules became applicable; enforcement powers started for applicable rules; Article 50 transparency obligations started to apply.
- 2 December 2026 — limited Article 50(2) transition for certain systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026, plus new prohibitions introduced by the amended framework.
- 2 December 2027 — amended timeline for Annex III high-risk AI systems.
- 2 August 2028 — amended timeline for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I.
